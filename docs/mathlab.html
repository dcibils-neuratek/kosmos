MathLab

A notebook of mathematics: type a function by hand and it is drawn, in two dimensions or three; ask a question of it and it is answered; and the processor's vector units do the arithmetic and say how much faster that was. And lessons of physics built from the same cells — how to launch a rocket, with the laws behind it drawn and the flight plotted. Drawn at the size the machine draws it before any of it is written (roadmap.md 4k).

The notebook

One column of cells, as a notebook has. A cell is a line typed by hand — in grey — shown set as mathematics above it, and what came of it underneath: a function of x is drawn as soon as it is typed, a function of x and y as a surface, plot puts several on one graph, and anything else is worked out. The last cell is where the next line goes. It works here as it will in the app: type in it and press Return, and drag a surface to turn it.

Lessons

Diego: "I want this to be a learning tool", "like how to launch a rocket", "and the physics behind", "and plot the functions and rocket trajectory", "so we can have learning lessons of physics", "and a graphical representation of the functions and laws". So a lesson is a notebook that explains as it goes: text between the cells, each law typed and set and drawn as a graph, sliders for the numbers that matter, the simulation itself, and questions to try. Every cell stays live, so a lesson can be changed and taken apart like any other notebook.

Who a lesson is for — Diego: "people that know some calculus but want to understand how things work so it needs to explain as much as possible". So a lesson derives its laws rather than stating them: every step a cell, every step said in words, the calculus shown where it does the work — as lesson 6 derives the rocket equation from momentum alone, and then counts what gravity and the air take from it.

Launching a rocket

A rocket carries its own push. It throws gas out of its back very fast, and the gas pushes back on the rocket just as hard — Newton's third law. As it burns, the rocket gets lighter, so the same push moves it faster and faster. This lesson works out how fast a rocket can go, and then flies one.

1

The push

Thrust is how much gas leaves each second, times how fast it leaves.

T = ṁ ve
T = mdot * ve
2

Newton's second law, for a mass that burns away

Thrust up, weight and air down; what is left accelerates the rocket.

m(t) a = T − m(t) g − ½ ρ(h) v2 Cd A
m(t) a = T - m(t) g - 1/2 rho(h) v^2 Cd A
3

The rocket equation

With no gravity and no air, the speed a rocket gains depends only on how fast its gas leaves and how much lighter it ends. The logarithm is why rockets are mostly fuel: ten times the mass at the start buys only 2.3 times the exhaust speed.

Δv = ve ln(m0/mf)
dv(r) = ve * ln(r), r = m0/mf

Where the rocket equation comes from

No law is assumed here but one: momentum is conserved. Watch the rocket for a moment dt, far from any planet and any air, so that nothing outside pushes on it.

4

At the start of the moment the rocket has mass m and speed v. During it, a little gas of mass dm leaves backwards at ve relative to the rocket, and the rocket, now m − dm, is going a little faster. The momentum before equals the momentum after:

m v = (m − dm)(v + dv) + dm (v − ve)
m v = (m - dm)(v + dv) + dm (v - ve)
5

Multiply out. The mv on each side cancels, and so do the v dm terms; dm dv is a small thing times a small thing, which vanishes as dt shrinks. What is left says that the gas's momentum backwards is the rocket's gain forwards. The mass the rocket loses is dm, so its own mass changes by dmrocket = −dm:

m dv = ve dm  ⇒  dv = −ve dmrocket / m
m dv = ve dm => dv = -ve dm_rocket / m
6

Add up every moment of the burn: integrate from the mass at launch, m0, to the mass when the fuel is gone, mf. The integral of 1/m is the logarithm — and there is the rocket equation of cell 3, with nothing assumed but momentum.

Δv = −ve ∫m0mf dm/m = ve ln(m0/mf)
dv = -ve * integral(1/m, m0, mf) = ve ln(m0/mf)

And why a real rocket gets less

The equation is for empty space. On a launch pad two things take their share while the engine burns: gravity, which pulls back for every second of the burn — so a rocket that burns slowly loses more — and the air, which pushes back harder the faster the rocket goes and the thicker the air is. Air thins by about a factor of e for every 8.5 km of height, which is why rockets go up first and sideways later. The flight below adds both up as it goes. A third, smaller share goes to pointing the engine where the rocket is not quite going: this one keeps its angle while its path bends, and only the part of the thrust along the path adds speed.

7
Δvreal = Δv − ∫0tb g sin γ dt − ∫0tb D/m dt,   ρ(h) = 1.225 e−h/8500
dv_real = dv - integral(g sin(gamma), 0, tb) - integral(D/m, 0, tb), rho(h) = 1.225 e^(-h/8500)

Try it

  1. Double the fuel. Does it go twice as high? Look at the graph in cell 3, and at what gravity took, for why not.
  2. Turn the air off. How much height did the air cost, and how much speed did it take (cell 7)?
  3. Tilt the rocket lower. Gravity takes less of the burn — but what happens to the air's share, and why?
  4. Take the fuel down until the facts say it cannot lift off. What is the thrust then, against the weight?
  5. Which launch angle carries it furthest? Is it the 45° that throws a ball furthest in lesson 3?
