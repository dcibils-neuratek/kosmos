# W2 - three things from Nebula, for JetSki

*Designed 11 November 2026 and agreed by Diego the same day ("yes to all").*
*Part 1, the futex, built in 0.11.369 (`testing.md` 18.764).*
JetSki is WebKit on Kosmos (`docs/webkit.md`). W1 ran C++ on Kosmos, and
everything above W1 needs three things the kernel does not have yet. Each
one is a change to Nebula, so each is here in detail: the calls, what they
mean, what they cost the kernel, and the test that proves them.

**In short.** A *futex*, so a thread waiting on a lock sleeps until the
lock is let go, not until the next tick. *Reserved address space*, so
JavaScriptCore can claim gigabytes of addresses, back them with pages
only where it touches, and mark pages read only or as guards. Writable and
executable are never allowed at once. And *a thread stopped and read*, so
the garbage collector can find every pointer another thread holds in its
registers.

None of this is a POSIX personality. There are no signals, no `mmap`
flags soup and no file descriptors. W3's POSIX layer, inside the process,
turns `pthread_mutex_lock`, `mmap` and `mprotect` into these calls, the way
`malloc` already turns into `SYS_MAP`.

## 1. The futex

**What JetSki needs.** WebKit's `WTF::Lock`, `Condition` and
`ParkingLot`, libc++'s `std::mutex`, and bmalloc's locks all come down to
one thing: *put me to sleep while this word still says what I saw, and
wake me when someone changes it.* Today Kosmos has no such call, so a
waiter sleeps for a whole tick and checks again (the malloc lock,
0.11.363). That puts a scheduler tick on every contended lock. WebKit, with
dozens of threads, would spend its life asleep.

**The calls.**

```
SYS_FUTEX_WAIT  (address, expected, wait_ticks)  -> 0, SYS_NO_CHANGE or SYS_TIMED_OUT
SYS_FUTEX_WAKE  (address, count)                 -> how many were woken
```

- `WAIT` reads the 32-bit word at `address`. If it is not `expected`, it
  returns `SYS_NO_CHANGE` at once: the lock moved before the caller could
  sleep. Otherwise the thread sleeps until a `WAKE` on the same address,
  or until `wait_ticks` passes (0 means no deadline). The deadline is
  converted once, by `thread_deadline_in`, as every timeout is.
- `WAKE` wakes up to `count` threads waiting on that address, oldest
  first, and says how many it woke. `count` of `~0` wakes them all.
- The address must be four-byte aligned, inside the process, and mapped
  writable. If not, the call answers `SYS_ERR_FAULT` and nothing sleeps.
  `SYS_NO_CHANGE` (-115) and `SYS_TIMED_OUT` (-116) are new codes, and
  like `SYS_NO_INPUT` they are answers rather than errors.

**The one rule that makes it correct.** The check and the sleep happen
under the same lock that `WAKE` takes. A waker always changes the word
*before* calling `WAKE`. So either the waiter's read sees the new value
and returns, or the waiter is already in the queue when `WAKE` looks.
There is no gap between the two where a wake can be lost.

**In the kernel.**
- **Where the waiters are kept.** A fixed table of 256 buckets, each a
  list head and a lock, in `kernel/futex.c`. Waiters are found by a hash
  of (address space, address).
- **No pool.** The waiter is the thread itself: `struct thread` gains a
  link, the key and a result. So there is no pool to size and nothing to
  run out of, which matches *no heap for kernel objects*.
- **Locks.** Each bucket's lock masks interrupts, as every lock does.
  Critical sections are a list walk.
- **Reading the word.** The kernel reads it through the process's own
  page tables, with the check `SYS_MEM` calls already use. A page given
  on touch is filled first, so a futex on fresh memory works.
- **A thread that ends while waiting** is taken out of its bucket by
  `thread_exit` and by the kill path, under the bucket's lock. The IPC
  bug W1d found was exactly the case where nobody did this.
- **Private to a process.** The key is (address space, address), so two
  processes cannot meet on a futex. A futex shared through a region would
  need (region, offset) as its key instead. Nothing asks for that yet, so
  it is not built (see *Not in W2*).

**Cost.** About 150 lines in `kernel/futex.c`, two syscalls, and three
words in `struct thread`.

**First users.** The libc's malloc lock drops its tick sleep, and
`kosmos_tls`'s thread start uses it. W3's pthreads are built on it.

**Test** (`tests/tests.c`, kernel suite, and a guest program):
1. Two threads share a counter behind a futex lock and each add 100,000.
   The total must be exact.
2. A waiter with a wrong `expected` returns `SYS_NO_CHANGE` without
   sleeping.
3. A waiter with `wait_ticks` 5 returns `SYS_TIMED_OUT` between 5 and 6
   ticks later.
4. `WAKE` with `count` 1 against three waiters wakes exactly one.
5. A thread killed while waiting leaves its bucket empty: the next `WAKE`
   answers 0 and the kernel does not fault.

**Control:** the same counter with `WAIT` built to skip its check of the
word loses wakeups. The harness must see it hang past its deadline, the
way W1d's control proved the unwinder.

**Measured** in the role, against the old lock in the same run: 5.3
times faster on x86-64 and 2.0 on AArch64, under TCG (`testing.md`
18.764).

## 2. Memory reserved, then committed and protected

**What JetSki needs.**
- **Reserving.** JavaScriptCore and bmalloc reserve big ranges of
  addresses up front: the JIT's executable pool, and the heap's
  *Gigacage*, which keeps arrays inside a known window so a bad index
  cannot reach anything else. They back pages only as they use them.
- **Protecting.** They mark pages read only or inaccessible, for guard
  pages below stacks and between arenas.
- **Today.** `SYS_MAP` gives zeroed pages eagerly, from a bump pointer,
  inside a 4 GB window, and every page is writable. None of the above is
  possible.

**The calls.**

```
SYS_RESERVE  (pages)                     -> address or error
SYS_PROTECT  (address, pages, access)    -> 0 or error
SYS_RELEASE  (address, pages)            -> 0 or error
```

`access` is one of:

| access | meaning |
|--------|---------|
| `NONE` | addresses only; touching one ends the process |
| `READ` | read only |
| `RW`   | read and write; filled with zeroed pages on first touch |
| `RX`   | read and execute |

- **`RESERVE`** gives a range of addresses with nothing behind it,
  `NONE` throughout. It costs the kernel one record and no pages. It does
  not count against `map_budget`.
- **`PROTECT`** changes the access of pages inside one reservation:
  - Making a page `RW` *commits* it: the first touch fills it with a
    zeroed page, as a region given on touch is filled today
    (`sharemap_fill`).
  - Making a page `NONE` takes its page back; it reads as zero if it is
    ever committed again.
  - `READ` and `RX` keep what the page holds.
  - Only committed pages count against `map_budget`, so a process that
    reserves 32 GB and touches 40 MB pays for 40 MB.
- **`RELEASE`** gives the addresses back and frees every page behind
  them.

**Writable and executable never at once.** There is no `RWX`, and asking
for it is `SYS_ERR_FLAGS`. A page can be written and *then* made `RX`, but
never both at the same time.

That is how JavaScriptCore works without `RWX`, and it has two ways to do
it:
- **flip**: write the code while the page is `RW`, then `PROTECT` it `RX`;
- **two mappings**: one region mapped twice, writable at an address only
  the JIT knows and executable at another. JavaScriptCore calls this the
  *separated W^X heap*, and it is the faster one.

The second needs one more thing: `SYS_MEM_MAP` gains an `RX` flag, so a
region already mapped `RW` can be mapped again executable. Both mappings
still obey W^X; each is one or the other.

**On AArch64** a page made `RX` needs its instruction cache made to agree
with what was written. EL0 can do this itself (`dc cvau` / `ic ivau`), if
`SCTLR_EL1.UCI` is set. W2 sets it, and the libc's
`__clear_cache` does the loop, as libgcc's does.

**Touching a `NONE` page ends the process.** There are no signals. The
fault message names the address and says it was a guard page, so the
cause is obvious. Nothing in WebKit needs to *survive* such a fault:
- JavaScriptCore's guard pages exist to stop a run-away, not to catch it;
- WebAssembly's "fast memory" is the one feature that catches faults on
  purpose, and it is off. Its bounds checks are compiled in instead,
  which is the configuration WebKit already supports.

**In the kernel.**
- **The window.** A third address window, `USER_RESERVE_VA`, carved out
  of the thread-stack window. Today stacks run from base + 8 GB to the
  end of the address space, half a million 1 MB slots. W2 caps them at
  64 GB, which is 57,344 threads per process, and gives the rest to
  reservations:
  - **AArch64: 192 GB** of reservable addresses, because its user
    space is 256 GB, from 256 GB to 512 GB, through three-level tables;
  - **x86-64: about 447 GB.**
  
  Both are far more than JavaScriptCore asks for, which is about 32 GB
  with the Gigacage on.
- **The records.** A reservation is a record in a pool that grows by
  slabs, like every kernel pool. It holds the range and, per page, two
  bits of access, kept in the level-3 page table entries themselves where
  possible. A `NONE` page is an invalid entry; a committed-on-touch `RW`
  page is an invalid entry whose software bits say "fill me". So the
  record stays the range alone, and the page tables are the truth.
- **Fault path.** `vm.c`'s user fault handler already asks
  `sharemap_fill` whether a fault is a page to fill. It asks the
  reservation table the same question.
- **TLB.** `PROTECT` that removes access shoots the page down on every
  core the process runs on. That uses the path x86 already has, and
  `tlbi` broadcast on AArch64.

**Cost.** About 400 lines, most of them in a new `kernel/reserve.c`. Three
syscalls. One flag on `SYS_MEM_MAP`. The fault path asks one more
question.

**Test:**
1. Reserve 16 GB, touch one page in a thousand. `sysinfo` must count only
   the touched pages.
2. Write a page, make it `READ`: reading gives back what was written, and
   writing ends the process with a guard-page message.
3. Make a page `RX` after writing an instruction sequence into it, then
   call it. It must return the expected value, on both architectures.
4. `PROTECT ... RWX` is refused.
5. Release, then reserve again 1,000 times. The pool and the page count
   must come back to where they started, which is what `make stress` asks
   of every resource.

**Control:** test 2 with `READ` built as `RW` must see the write succeed
and the suite fail.

## 3. A thread suspended and its registers read

**What JetSki needs.** JavaScriptCore's garbage collector is
*conservative* about stacks. To collect, it stops every thread that runs
JavaScript, reads each one's registers and stack, and treats anything
that looks like a pointer into the heap as live. On Darwin it does this
with `thread_suspend` and `thread_get_state`; on Linux, with a signal
handler that parks the thread. Kosmos has neither.

**The calls.**

```
SYS_THREAD_SUSPEND  (index)            -> 0 or error
SYS_THREAD_REGS     (index, buffer)    -> 0 or error
SYS_THREAD_RESUME   (index)            -> 0 or error
```

- **Only the caller's own threads.** `index` names a thread in the
  caller's own process, the same index `SYS_THREAD_CREATE` returned. A
  process cannot suspend another process's thread, because it has no way
  to name one.
- **`SUSPEND`** returns only once the thread is off every processor.
  - If it is running on another core, that core is asked to
    reschedule (the IPI the preemption path already sends), and the
    caller waits for it to leave.
  - If it is blocked in a call, it stays blocked, and it does not return
    to user code until it is resumed.
  - Suspends count: two `SUSPEND`s need two `RESUME`s. A thread cannot
    suspend itself (`SYS_ERR_DENIED`), which is how a collector could
    deadlock.
- **`REGS`** copies the thread's saved user registers into `buffer`, in
  a declared shape, `struct kosmos_regs` in `user/include/threadproto.h`:
  - on x86-64: the sixteen general registers, `rip` and `rflags`;
  - on AArch64: `x0`-`x30`, `sp`, `pc` and `pstate`.
  
  These are exactly what the trap frame already holds. Vector registers
  are not copied: JavaScriptCore does not keep pointers in them. Doing so
  would also make lazy FP save write them back first.
- **`RESUME`** lets it run again when the count reaches zero.

**In the kernel.**
- **New state.** `struct thread` gains a suspend count. The scheduler
  will not pick a thread whose count is above zero. A blocked thread
  woken while suspended goes back to sleep at the wake, rather than onto
  a runqueue.
- **Locking.** This uses the thread's home core's runqueue lock, and the
  `on_cpu` wait that `thread_move` already has (`testing.md` 18.612):
  that is the "has it left the processor yet" question, answered once
  already.
- **Kill.** A suspended thread that is killed simply dies; killing does
  not need it to run.

**Cost.** About 120 lines, three syscalls, one field.

**Test:**
1. A thread spins on a counter. `SUSPEND`, read the counter twice a tick
   apart: it has not moved. `RESUME`: it moves again.
2. The spinning thread keeps a known value in a callee-saved register
   (`x19` / `rbx`, set in a few lines of assembly). `REGS` must return
   it, and `pc` inside the spin loop.
3. A thread blocked in `SYS_FUTEX_WAIT`, suspended, then woken: it must
   not return from the wait until resumed.
4. `SUSPEND` on itself, or on an index that is not its own, is refused.

**Control:** test 1 with `SUSPEND` built not to wait for `on_cpu` must
see the counter move, and the suite fail.

## What each part costs Nebula, together

| part | new syscalls | about | new kernel state |
|------|--------------|-------|------------------|
| futex | 2 | 150 lines | 256 buckets; 3 words a thread |
| reserve / protect | 3, and a flag | 400 lines | a growing pool of ranges; software bits in page tables |
| suspend / registers | 3 | 120 lines | a count a thread |

**What Nebula still does, and nothing else.** Threads, address spaces, IPC
and capabilities. All three parts are about threads and address spaces,
and there is no file, socket or signal in any of them. `make size` will
move by about 670 lines. That is what belongs there needing more code,
which the smoke alarm paragraph in `CLAUDE.md` says is not a problem.

**Order.** The futex first: smallest, and malloc wants it today. Then
reserve and protect. Then suspension, which needs a thread to be blocked
in something worth suspending, and the futex is that.

Each part is its own revision, with its suites, before the next.

## Not in W2

- **A futex shared between processes** (keyed by region and offset).
  WebKit's processes talk by IPC and shared surfaces, not by sharing
  locks; build it when something wants it.
- **Signals, or catching a fault.** See *Touching a `NONE` page* above.
  The one WebKit feature that wants it, WebAssembly fast memory, is off.
- **A 48-bit address space on AArch64** (four levels of tables). Its
  192 GB is enough for JetSki, and changing the depth of the tables is
  `arch/` work of its own. It is on the roadmap if a later engine wants
  more.

## What is Diego's to decide

1. **The design as a whole**: these eight syscalls and one flag, with the
   semantics above.
2. **The stack window capped at 64 GB** (57,344 threads per process), to
   make room for reservations. The alternative is reservations in the 4
   GB `SYS_MAP` window, which is too small for JavaScriptCore's Gigacage,
   or the Gigacage turned off, which loses a defence.
3. **The JIT's W^X by two mappings** (an `RX` flag on `SYS_MEM_MAP`)
   rather than flipping protection, which is slower. Both obey "never
   writable and executable at once".
