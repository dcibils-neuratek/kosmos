A page, laid out

This document exists so that a screenshot of the browser proves something. Every block on it is one the renderer handles differently, so a picture of it shows at a glance which of them are working and which are not.

Headings step down

An h1 is set at twenty-eight pixels and an h2 at twenty-two, both bold, and both carry a rule underneath. An h3 shares eighteen pixels with everything below it, because a document that needs six distinct heading sizes is a document with a structure problem.

And this is one of those

The paragraph face is IBM Plex Sans at sixteen pixels. Accented Latin is here on purpose - naïve, Ångström, Grüße, Málaga, œuvre, çedilla - because the text path decodes UTF-8 and rasterises a glyph the first time it is asked for, and a row of boxes here would mean neither of those is true.

Inline, which is what boxes bought

A word can be bold, or italic, or monospace, in the middle of a sentence set in none of those - and the four sit on one shared baseline rather than on a shared top edge, which is the arithmetic that stops mixed faces looking subtly broken. This is a link, and following it is the whole difference between a document viewer and a browser.

  • A list item is indented by twenty-four pixels, with a marker.
  • It wraps at the same width as everything else, which is what makes a hanging indent visible: the second line of a long item lines up under the first rather than under the margin.
  • The marker is a disc for every item, which is wrong for an ol - list-style comes from the cascade, and the cascade is not consulted here yet.
A quotation is set in italic, indented by thirty-two pixels, and inked a little lighter than the body. Three separate signals for one idea, which is what a quotation on a page has always been.

What is not here

There is no box model, no floats, no width, and no colour from the cascade: the stylesheet at the top of this file parses, and nothing on this page is drawn from it. Faces are chosen by tag. That is the next piece of work and the page says so rather than pretending otherwise.

  a monospace block
  keeps its spaces
  and its line breaks

That one works because a box knows which block it belongs to. The other page says what is still missing.

Long enough to scroll

The rest of this page exists so that there is something below the fold. A page laid out into a surface as tall as the whole document and scrolled by copying a band out of it is the reason the browser draws its own pixels, and a test page that fits in the window would check none of that.

Scrolling here re-runs nothing. The document was laid out once when it arrived: every line broken, every glyph rasterised, every block placed. What a keypress changes is which row of that surface is copied to the top of the window, which is one blit and no arithmetic anybody can get wrong.

What the alternative would have cost

Laying out on every scroll means breaking two thousand words and drawing several thousand glyphs, sixty times a second, for a picture that differs from the last one by forty pixels. That is not a slow implementation of scrolling; it is the wrong shape, and it is the same wrong shape the PDF viewer avoided for the same reason.

The surface is the caller's and is scrolled by blitting out of it, which is why this lays a page out once into something taller than the window rather than re-laying it on every scroll.

There is a ceiling, and it is honest about itself: sixteen megabytes of paper, which at this width is about seven screenfuls. A document taller than that is cut off, and the status line says by how much rather than letting the page simply stop.
